CanSat Bahrain 2026  ·  the plan prepared 12 October 2026

What the project is, and a plan

Source: [CanSat Bahrain – 2026] Guidelines.pdf (10 pages, BSA + AUBH) + the promo infographic. Prepared 12 October 2026.

1. The project in one paragraph

Your team designs, builds, tests and flies a satellite the size of a soda can. All the subsystems of a real satellite — power, communications, sensors, payload, and a parachute recovery system — have to fit inside a 66 mm × 115 mm cylinder (~350 ml). On launch day a BSA expert drops it from a tower/high building; it must collect and transmit data on the way down, land safely under its parachute, and you then present the mission results to a jury of Bahrain Space Agency engineers. It is a compressed version of a real space mission lifecycle: concept → requirements → design reviews → integration & test → launch → data analysis → results presentation. Commitment: ~8 hours per week, 25 Oct → 12 Dec 2026 (that is the stated figure; expect 15–25 h/week in the last two weeks).

3. Mission design — what actually wins points

Primary mission (the baseline every team does): measure and transmit the descent profile — pressure/altitude, temperature, humidity, IMU attitude, compass heading — plus images during descent. Success criterion = complete, correctly-united data set down to landing.

Secondary mission = your differentiator. The guidelines explicitly reward (a) creative use of the camera and (b) creative additions/enhancements to the kit (better parachute, better 3D model, extra components). Pick one that produces a measurement, not just a photo:

Option Why it scores Cost / risk
A. Parachute aerodynamics study — two parachute geometries (flat vs hemispherical vs cross), quantify descent rate, oscillation amplitude, rotation rate Directly names "improved parachute design" as a reward; cheap; pure engineering science; big data set from kit sensors alone Needs 2+ builds and repeat drop tests
B. Vertical atmospheric profiling over Bahrain — temperature/humidity/pressure lapse-rate profile, correlations with ground station data Clean scientific interest, easy to argue relevance (heat, humidity, dust) Science can feel generic; needs good analysis + references
C. Camera-based ground/aerosol imaging — stitch descent images into a mosaic, classify surface type / detect haze layers Maximises the "creative use of the camera" criterion Image handling on a Nano is the hard part
D. Add an air-quality/gas sensor (CO₂ or PM2.5) for a vertical pollution profile Kit addition + real local relevance + a plot judges can read instantly Extra mass/power; sensor must tolerate the thermal environment

Recommendation for a 7-week timeline: primary = full descent profile telemetry at 1 Hz + onboard logging; secondary = A or D (A if the team is mechanical/software-strong, D if they can get a sensor quickly). Both give a real independent variable, real graphs, and a clean "did the mission succeed?" answer. Avoid a secondary mission that is just "take pretty pictures" — it scores under camera creativity but contributes little to the 35 % science block.

Meanwhile, lock the boring things early (they are 25 % of the score): set up the outreach page in week 1, keep a decision log and a test log with dates, and label every graph with units.

                            |  microSD module  <-- ADD THIS    Ground station: 433 MHz LoRa RX
                            |  (onboard backup log)            + Arduino/PC + laptop logging at 1 Hz
                            +---+-------------------------------+
                                |
                          [battery: 1S LiPo / AA pack]
                                |
                         [parachute recovery system]

Non-negotiable design decisions to make at MCR stage: 1. Telemetry at 1 Hz + onboard SD logging. Never trust the radio link alone; a lost packet must not mean a lost mission. (A microSD module is a few dollars — an explicit "kit addition".) 2. Ground station receiver. The kit lists a LoRa transmitter only. Confirm with the organisers whether a matched receiver is provided; if not, budget for the same 433 MHz module plus an Arduino/PC on the ground, and test range before flight. 3. Power budget & mass budget — spreadsheet from day one. The Nano's 2 KB RAM and 32 KB flash constrain the code; images must be streamed/stored, never buffered in RAM. 4. Camera interface is the #1 unknown. A Nano cannot drive a normal parallel/MIPI camera. Get the exact module model from the organisers at the 25 Oct workshop and design around it. 5. Recovery system — parachute sized for a target descent rate (design for ~4–7 m/s), with a shock cord and a container that ejects cleanly. Test with repeated drops from a height (school building, drone, crane) — at minimum 5 successful drops before 2 Dec.

Top risks: camera/Nano integration; LoRa range and antenna placement inside a metal-free envelope; battery voltage sag under camera load; parachute tangling; timeline compression at CDR; data corrupted by vibration. Mitigate each with a bench test you can point to in a review.

6. "Can I use AI for it?"

Straight answer: yes — the guidelines contain no restriction on AI or CAD/EDA/software tools, and the judges are grading the result and your understanding of it, not your editor history. But the way AI helps and the way it hurts are both easy to predict here:

Where AI genuinely helps (use it, no hesitation) - Learning the sensors fast: "explain the BMP180 I²C register map and give me a working Arduino read function", datasheet triage, unit conversions, I²C address conflicts. - Code: Arduino firmware scaffolding, telemetry packet format, CRC, SD logging, Python analysis scripts (pandas/matplotlib with correct axis labels and units), ground-station code. - Mechanical: OpenSCAD/parametric can structure, parachute sizing maths, drag/descent-rate calcs. - Documents and decks: turning your own notes into a review-ready structure, editing the Pre-Launch report, checking that every graph has units, building the outreach page. - Rehearsal: generating the hostile judge questions for each review is one of the highest-value uses — 3 minutes of Q&A is graded.

Where AI will cost you marks if you lean on it - 35 % of the score is scientific value, and "scientific understanding" is explicitly judged in the Q&A. A jury of BSA engineers will ask why you chose an equation or a filter. If nobody on the team can derive it, that block collapses. - 25 % is "professional competencies", including technical reporting. A report you cannot defend is worth less than a simpler report you can. - AI cannot solder, fit, test, drop, fly or recover. At 8 h/week the schedule is the binding constraint, and no model shortens a physical test campaign. - AI will confidently produce wrong science (fabricated units, invented constants, plausible but wrong datasheet values). Every AI-sourced number must be checked against the datasheet or a textbook before it enters a review deck. - School rules still apply: check the university's academic-integrity policy and your supervisor's stance on AI-assisted work, and disclose its use if asked. Using AI is normal engineering; misrepresenting someone else's work as your own is not.

Working rule: use AI for typing, formatting, boilerplate, translation of jargon and rehearsal. Keep the reasoning, the mission objectives, the design justification and the physical build inside the team — and make sure someone can explain every design decision without notes.

7. Week-by-week plan (Mon 12 Oct → Sat 12 Dec 2026)

Week 0 — by Fri 17 Oct: REGISTER (hard deadline) Recruit the exact 5 + supervisor · draft mission theme bullets for the Dean · get the Letter of Endorsement signed · submit the Google Form · email events@bsa.bh for Appendix I templates, the exact camera module model, and whether a LoRa ground receiver is supplied.

Week 1 (18–24 Oct): build the ground base Create the shared drive, decision log, mass/power budget, outreach page. Assign the 5 roles. Read all sensor datasheets. Start the mission-concept narrative (why this mission matters).

Week 2 (25–28 Oct): MCR — due 28 Oct Workshop on 25 Oct (get the kit, confirm camera + LoRa). Objective: freeze the mission statement, primary & secondary objectives, success criteria, top-level requirements, and a concept block diagram. Rehearse 3 min of Q&A.

Weeks 3–4 (29 Oct – 11 Nov): PDR + first hardware Breadboard the full sensor chain on the Nano. Working telemetry to a PC. Pixel-level design of the mechanical structure, parachute sizing, power and mass budgets. First drop tests with a dummy. Write the requirements-to-design traceability table. PDR deck due 11 Nov.

Weeks 5–6 (12–18 Nov): CDR — freeze the design Full schematic + functional block diagram + mechanical drawings. Firmware feature-complete on breadboard, data pipeline producing real plots. Test plan written (bench, drop, range, thermal). CDR deck due 18 Nov. Stop changing architecture after this review.

Weeks 7–8 (19 Nov – 2 Dec): integration & test, SIR/FRR Solder the flight board, fit everything into the can envelope, weigh it, run the test campaign: ≥5 drop tests, LoRa range test, end-to-end mission simulation (drop → log → plot). Fix everything the tests break. SIR/FRR deck + Pre-Launch report due 2 Dec. This is the gate to fly.

Week 9 (3–9 Dec): flight readiness 6 Dec session; 7–8 Dec full dress rehearsal with the final CanSat, fresh battery, spares kit. Final results presentation pre-built with placeholders (launch results, plots, conclusions, challenges, lessons learned, outreach summary). 9 Dec: sign-off + hand over the CanSat.

Week 10 — 12 Dec: LAUNCH Arrive with the CanSat flight-ready, spare parts, printed checksheets and half the deck already done. Fly, recover, fill in the plots within the hour, present in launch order, 7 min + Q&A.
